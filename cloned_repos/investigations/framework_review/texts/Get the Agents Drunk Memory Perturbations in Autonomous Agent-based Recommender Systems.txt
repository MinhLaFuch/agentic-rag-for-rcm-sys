ABSTRACT
Large language model-based agents are increasingly used in recommender systems (Agent4RSs) to achieve personalized behavior modeling. Specifically, Agent4RSs introduces memory mechanisms that enable the agents to autonomously learn and self-evolve from real-world interactions. However, to the best of our knowledge, how robust Agent4RSs are remains unexplored. As such, in this paper, we propose the first work to attack Agent4RSs by perturbing agents' memories, not only to uncover their limitations but also to enhance their security and robustness, ensuring the development of safer and more reliable AI agents.
Given the security and privacy concerns, it is more practical to launch attacks under a black-box setting, where the accurate knowledge of the victim models cannot be easily obtained. Moreover, the practical attacks are often stealthy to maximize the impact. To this end, we propose a novel practical attack framework named DrunkAgent. DrunkAgent consists of a generation module, a strategy module, and a surrogate module. The generation module aims to produce effective and coherent adversarial textual triggers, which can be used to achieve attack objectives such as promoting the target items. The strategy module is designed to `get the target agents drunk' so that their memories cannot be effectively updated during the interaction process. As such, the triggers can play the best role. Both of the modules are optimized on the surrogate module to improve the transferability and imperceptibility of the attacks. By identifying and analyzing the vulnerabilities, our work provides critical insights that pave the way for building safer and more resilient Agent4RSs. Extensive experiments across various real-world datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of DrunkAgent.

1 INTRODUCTION
Recommender systems (RSs) have evolved into a vital component of the modern digital ecosystem. From e-commerce sites (e.g., Amazon, Taobao) and streaming platforms (e.g., YouTube, TikTok) to social media networks (e.g., Facebook, Instagram), RSs power personalized experience, drive user engagement of these online platforms, and further influence market dynamics through item promotions [45, 47]. The foundation of the RSs lies in modeling users' preferences based on their historical interactions with items, such as purchases and ratings, known as collaborative filtering (CF). Despite the success of CF techniques [14, 15, 19], they often struggle with data sparsity and cold-start problems [30]. To alleviate the problems, the auxiliary information with rich semantics (e.g., item descriptions and user reviews) has been integrated into RSs [52]. Given the increasing volume and complexity of recommendation data, the RSs encounter difficulties in scaling effectively and capturing textual knowledge about users and items sufficiently, due to they generally rely on traditional deep learning (DL) models like CNNs and RNNs [56].
Recently, large language models (LLMs) have shown potential to mitigate the above problems and revolutionize RSs [56], as they are primarily built upon advanced transformer-based architectures and pretrained on diverse and extensive datasets. Most of works

However, the LLMbased RSs (LLM4RSs) struggle to capture underlying behavioral patterns, due to the gap between universal language modeling and personalized behavior modeling [50]. Integrating agents built upon LLMs equipped with additional modules offers a promising solution [10, 16], allowing to autonomously explore and learn from external sources. Among all the added modules, memory is a key component to support agent-environment1 interactions, which plays a crucial role in helping agents accumulate historical experience, retrieve informative knowledge and self-evolve in real-world environments [36, 55]. Consequently, the agent-based RSs with powerful LLMs [16, 50] can also leverage memory modules that store the information explored from the external environments to further improve the recommendation quality. Fig. 1 gives an example of an item agent interacting with its situated environment (e.g., users and other items), where the learning knowledge will be placed in its memory. Despite the aforementioned success, the safety vulnerability of the generative agent-based RSs (Agent4RSs) under adversarial attacks has yet to be explored. Such attacks can gain insights into the robustness of the systems and help develop effective defensive mechanisms. As recommender agents are integrated into more complex real-world environments, understanding how these attacks might compromise their behaviors is critical for building trustworthy and robust recommender agents [6].
Due to security and privacy considerations, the access of accurate knowledge of the victim model, including architectures, gradients, parameters, etc., is generally restricted in practice. As such, practical attacks are mainly under a black-box setting [23, 28, 45, 48, 51]. Practical attacks are often required to have transferability (i.e.,

Conventional black-box shilling attacks [38], which inject wellcrafted fake user profiles (e.g., a set of users' numerical ratings on items) into the victim model for certain attack objectives (e.g., promote their target items for profits), have been shown to effectively poison DL-based RSs. However, most attacks have difficulty in processing textual inputs (e.g., item titles and descriptions) and lack context awareness, making it challenging to effectively target LLM-empowered RSs that rely primarily on text inputs to generate natural language responses [28]. Item providers are generally responsible for creating and maintaining their own product representations on the platforms with recommendation services in practice [7, 26, 45] (e.g., YouTube allows creators to upload item titles and Amazon enables sellers to upload item descriptions), and current RSs depend heavily on these representations [51, 56]. As such, security vulnerabilities in these systems naturally emerge. The vulnerabilities can easily be exploited by profit-driven merchants manipulating product representations, as shown in Fig. 1. Compared to the shilling attacks that involve hiring online writers to introduce fake reviews to indirectly promote the items, this type of attack is more direct, efficient, and lower in economic cost [51].
Recent black-box textual attacks on LLM4RSs are far from practical. Classical text attacks that are successful in text misclassifications, such as TextFooler [18] and PuncAttack [11], have been directly applied for LLM4RSs [51]. However, the effectiveness of the attacks is limited, due to there is a gap between the attack goals of the text attacks and RS attacks. Trivial and ChatGPT attacks [51], on the other hand, are proposed to be tailored for LLM4RSs, which increases the items' attractiveness by appending positive words to the titles. Nevertheless, such attacks are not optimized on any black-box RSs, making them poorly transferable to the Agent4RSs. Moreover, since the attacks mainly target modifications to item titles, where titles' brevity and frequent exposure to users tend to make even small perturbations noticeable.
To tackle the above problems, in this paper, we propose a new practical attack framework named DrunkAgent. Instead of manipulating the item titles, to enhance the imperceptibility of attacks, we propose to perturb the description of the target item, where length and context can easily hide changes. DrunkAgent is composed of three modules: generation module, strategy module and surrogate module. The generation module is designed to produce effective adversarial textual description triggers, which can be used to promote the target items to as many normal users as possible on the agentpowered RSs. The triggers are coherent, semantically meaningful and human-readable text, making the attack stealthy. The strategy module aims to `get the target agents drunk', that is, to confuse the agents' memory mechanisms so that they cannot be effectively updated during agent-environment interactions. In this way, the triggers can play the best role. To avoid frequently querying the victim models to induce suspicion and mimic the black-box settings in the real world, the generation module and the strategy module are optimized on the surrogate module to improve the transferability and imperceptibility of the practical attacks.

Beyond demonstrating the attack capabilities of DrunkAgent, our broader goal is to reveal and understand the overlooked vulnerabilities in Agent4RSs. Specifically, by analyzing how agents' memory mechanisms can be influenced through carefully crafted and stealthy textual triggers, our work provides a new lens into the emerging attack surfaces introduced by memory-augmented, LLM-based agents. We believe that this exploration can offer valuable insights to the community for designing more resilient agent architectures, guiding future efforts on defense mechanisms such as memory validation or input sanitization.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
· We identify a safety vulnerability in autonomous agent-based RSs with powerful LLMs. To the best of our knowledge, we present the first formal and systematic study of adversarial textual attacks on the agent-powered RSs, aiming to inform future robustness research.
· We propose to perturb the description of the target item for effective memory perturbations, which is an unnoticeable and crucial property of current agentic recommendations.
· We propose a novel practical attack framework on the agentpowered RSs named DrunkAgent, which can create adversarial descriptions of target items with transferability and imperceptibility.
· Our DrunkAgent significantly outperforms state-of-the-art black-box attacks against various LLM-based agentic RSs on real-world datasets in terms of transferability and imperceptibility. Moreover, state-of-the-art defense strategies are ineffective to DrunkAgent, demonstrating the need for improved defense mechanisms to guide more secure Agent4RSs.

2.2 Threat Model
2.2.1 Attacker's Knowledge: As the works [23, 28, 45, 48, 51] point out, the inherent details (e.g., the architectures, gradients and parameters) of the victim RSs often cannot be obtained in practice. As such, our attack is performed under the black-box settings. In addition, the attacker is only able to access publicly available recommendation data (e.g., item titles and user reviews that can be collected directly by browsing or writing crawlers), due to the openness nature of RSs [31, 47].
2.2.2 Attacker's Capability: Unlike another state-of-the-art LLMpowered RS attack [28] with an unrealistic assumption of P and V can be easily perturbed (that will be detailed in Section 5), we consider that the attackers have limited perturbation capability in practice. Specifically, they can only interfere with the representation of the target item, which is feasible and can be easily accomplished via a dedicated API, as the existing practical attacks do [26, 45, 51].
2.2.3 Attacker's Objectives: As mentioned in Section 1, two objectives: transferability and imperceptibility are considered for the practical attack. Given that push attacks are generally used by unscrupulous parties on RSs for profits in practice [31] with the aim of promoting target items to as many normal users as possible [53], we thus focus on such attacks in this paper, as normally do in recent works [38, 51]. Our first goal is to maximize the hit ratio of the target item  on a surrogate RS (denoted as Agent with parameters ) that is different from the victim black-box RS, ensuring transferability. Our second goal is to make the descriptions of  as stealthy as possible, achieving imperceptibility. As such, the surrogate RS is used to optimize attacks to avoid frequently querying the victim models to induce suspicion.
3 DRUNKAGENT
In this section, we present a practical attack framework, DrunkAgent, for gaining insights into the robustness of autonomous agent-based RSs with powerful LLMs and related defense models in practice. DrunkAgent is composed of three attack modules. Fig. 2 gives an overview of DrunkAgent, where each component will be described in the following.
3.1 Surrogate Module
We leverage a surrogate RS  to measure how effective the attack is under the black-box settings, as inspired by the existing works [23, 32, 45, 47]. It is used to optimize the generation module and the strategy module, making the attack transferable and imperceptible.
Since our attack targets SOTA LLM-powered RSs, we adopt powerful LLMs as recommendation agents. Given the transparency, flexibility and scalability of the open-source LLM models, they can be considered for building this module. Moreover, due to our attack focus on adversarial text description generation tasks, we consider an autoregressive language model trained with a causal language modeling objective [5, 29]. There are some notable models, such as LLaMA [33], LLaMA 2 [34] and LLaMA 3 [9], BLOOM 176B [20] and Falcon 180B [3]. 3.2 Generation Module
In this section, we aim to generate adversarial description triggers on the agent-powered RSs, which can promote the target items to as many normal users as possible. The triggers should have three critical properties: 1) Universality on any users and items; 2) Transferability among different black-box victim models; and 3) Imperceptibility to not easier to be discovered. To this end, we design sub-modules, as shown in Fig. 2 and described as follows.
3.2.1 Adversarial Prompting Engineering. Recently, many of the mainstream LLMs that have been widely used in the agentic recommendation community [16] (e.g., GPT-3.5-turbo, ChatGPT) are closed source, where the models' underlying codes, weights, or other critical components are not released to the public and the models can only be invoked through their APIs. As a result, the agent-powered RSs generally adopt in-context learning (ICL) [8] to sufficiently take advantage of the generalization capability of LLMs on unknown tasks. To this end, this module carefully crafts prompts X within the ICL framework to improve the quality of adversarial trigger generation.
Basic Prompt Template. We meticulously prepare prompt templates P to provide context, goals, instructions and constraints so

that the model knows `what needs to be done' and `how to respond to'. The main components are described in the following.
(1) Style Definition  . There are two common styles of prompts: task-oriented and role-oriented [57]. To make recommendations more authentic and reasonable, we introduce role information into the context, which allows the system to respond with domain-specific knowledge, such as `You are a CD enthusiast.', as shown in Fig. 2.
(2) Task Goal . Since a recommender system is essentially a ranker [52], we set sorting goals to indicate recommendation tasks, such as `Now, you are considering to sort ten candidate CDs in order of how well they align with your preferences.' in the given example of the figure.
(3) Recommendation Instruction  . We introduce highly detailed instructions to guide the model to perform accurate recommendations. For example, `Please sort CDs by considering the correlation between your preferen -ces/dislikes and the features of the CDs. The higher the CD rank, the more you like it.' in the shown example.
(4) Format Constraint  . We give constraints on the response format like `Your output should be in the format: The sorted CDs are:\n1. [Title · · · the least favorite CD]' to reduce undesirable outputs and facilitate subsequent optimizations.

3.2.2 Greedy Search Algorithm. We propose a greedy search algorithm to optimize the adversarial description triggers, as inspired by the successes of the current jailbreak attacks on LLMs [24, 59]. However, there are some essential differences (e.g., attack goals and victim models) between jailbreak attacks and our attacks. Jailbreak attacks are designed to undermine the LLMs' human-aligned values or other constraints imposed by model developers, compelling them to respond to malicious questions posed by adversaries with correct answers rather than rejection, whereas our attacks aim to promote the target items on the RSs powered by autonomous agents.

4 EXPERIMENTS
4.1 Experimental Setup
4.1.1 Datasets Selection. We use three widely-adopted and textintensive datasets containing real-world data in the current recommendation studies [16] to comprehensively evaluate DrunkAgent. They are CDs & Vinyl, Office Products and Musical Instruments of Amazon Review Data [27].
4.1.2 Baseline Attack Methods. Since we are the first work to gain insight into the robustness of the generative agent-powered RSs, there is no any baselines. To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of DrunkAgent, we follow the state-of-the-art attack on LLM-based RSs [51] and compare DrunkAgent with the benign status and six black-box attack methods, where the perturbations range from character-level: DeepwordBug [12] and PuncAttack [11],

word-level: TextFooler [18] and BertAttack [21] to sentence-level: TrivialInsertion and ChatGPTAttack [51]. To maintain a fair comparison, the methods perform perturbations on the descriptions of the target items and are optimized by the surrogate model that is the same as DrunkAgent.
4.1.3 Targeted Recommender Systems. We adopt three different victim agentic RSs to evaluate the effectiveness of DrunkAgent under the practical settings: AgentCF [50], retrieval-augmented AgentCF (AgentRAG) and sequential AgentCF (AgentSEQ), where the agent's backbone LLM is gpt-3.5-turbo2. Given the context window constraints pointed in [50], a target item and nine noninteractive items of each normal user are randomly selected for the ranking task of the RSs. To ensure the high-quality of recommendations, we introduce Chain-of-Thought into the victim RSs. As such, the victim RSs are totally different from as well as more powerful and context-rich than the surrogate model, which can maintain a strict black-box setting for sufficient evaluations.
4.1.4 Evaluation Metrics. We use two widely-used ranking metrics to evaluate the attack transferability: hit ratio (HR@K) and normalized discounted cumulative gain (NDCG@K) [39, 47]. To clearly show the performance gap among different attacks, we set K to 1, 2, 3. For the attack imperceptibility, we adopt standard sentence perplexity score [51] as the metric.
4.1.5 Configuration of DrunkAgent. As discussed in Section 3.1, we apply the latest open-source model LLaMA 3 for the surrogate model. Its collection spans different sizes, from 8B, 70B to 405B. To enhance the efficiency and ensure the quality of the attack, we use

4.4 Attack Imperceptibility
4.4.1 Overall Imperceptibility. Following the works [25, 51], we assess the attack imperceptibility by the text perplexity of GPTNeo. From Fig. 4, compared to other attacks, we can find that DrunkAgent has better imperceptibility by achieving lower perplexity. This also shows that the adversarial descriptions generated from DrunkAgent are high-quality, which is not only readable and fluent but also stealthy and semantically meaningful.

To further evaluate the attack stealthiness [51], we give a distribution of recommendation performance differences between the models before and after the attacks. The distribution includes various victim models and real-world datasets. To clearly demonstrate the differences, we use HR@3 to evaluate, as shown in Fig. 5. From the figure, we can find that DrunkAgent does not induce drastic changes in the overall performance, which indicates its unnoticeable.

Following the works [49, 51], we use the paraphrasing defensive strategy (Para.) via OpenAI-GPT-o1 to combat the attacks of agentic models. As can be seen from Fig. 6, DrunkAgent is still transferable, which shows that DrunkAgent is robust to such defenses. Moreover, DrunkAgent still performs better than the baselines after defenses were introduced, as shown in Fig. 6 and Table 1. Interestingly, paraphrasing enhances our attack to a certain extent, e.g., DrunkAgent with Para. is more effective than DrunkAgent without Para. on Office Products. 5 RELATED WORKS
In recent years, shilling attacks (aka data poisoning attacks) on conventional RSs [17, 22, 23, 31, 47, 48] have been fully investigated to perform resilience analysis of RSs. However, such attacks interfere the training process of models by injecting fake user profiles, which have become less effective even of limited applicable to the recent RSs that are empowered by LLMs. The main reasons are: 1) LLM-powered RSs either leverage LLM's in-context learning capabilities or fine-tune the LLMs with very little data to improve recommendations [56], which is not required to retrain the models; 2) there are textual semantic gaps between such attacks and current models [28]. Hence, there is an urgent need to investigate a novel textual attack paradigm mainly tailored for the inference phase of models [51]. There are only two state-of-the-art LLM4RS attacks in the literature [28, 51], but they are far from practical.
Zhang et al. propose a novel black-box attack [51], where attackers can significantly boost a target item's exposure by merely altering its textual content during the testing. Compared with traditional shilling attacks, such an item representation attack is notably stealthy [51], as it does not affect the overall recommendation performance, and it should be more efficient due to it perturbs the item features directly. However, its transferability is limited, due to the attack is not optimized on any black-box RSs [17, 45]. Moreover, the attack focuses on perturbing the titles, making them easier to be discovered. Meanwhile, another black-box attack CheatAgent [28] has been proposed. However, this work perturbs prompt templates and user profiles of victim LLM4RSs by harnessing LLM-based agents, which is impractical. The main reasons are: 1) tampering with prompt templates may induce the overall performance changes, making the attack perceptible [51]; and 2) it is difficult to inject interaction records into the specific positions of user profiles, because historical interactions are usually introduced into prompts in chronological order [50]. Furthermore, CheatAgent is an untargeted attack that undermines the model's overall performance, which is beyond the scope of this paper, due to we focus on targeted attacks. In addition, as far as we know, there is no work investigating on textual attacks against the current agent-powered RSs.
To tackle the above problems, we propose a practical attack framework, DrunkAgent, for the agent-based RSs with the aim of promoting target items. Our attack perturbs items' descriptions to

improve its effectiveness and imperceptibility, where the descriptions are optimized on black-box RSs to enhance its transferability.
6 CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose a novel practical attack framework, DrunkAgent, for autonomous agent-powered RSs with powerful LLMs. DrunkAgent launches attacks by carefully crafting adversarial descriptions of the target items on agentic recommendations. In this way, the memories of the target agents can be effectively perturbed for achieving the attack objectives. Comprehensive experiments on real-world datasets show that DrunkAgent achieves state-ofthe-art transferability and imperceptibility under the black-box settings. Most importantly, we identify a new safety vulnerability in the current agent4RSs. Our findings not only demonstrate an effective attack method, but also provide a lens to examine how memory mechanisms in agent-based recommenders can be influenced through real-world inputs. Our work can facilitate a deeper understanding of agent vulnerabilities and guide future efforts in building more robust and trustworthy recommendation agents. Since the generative agent4RS is rapidly flourishing in the recommendation community, we hope this work can be considered for building next-generation robust RSs in the future.

A APPENDIX
A.1 DrunkAgent Algorithm
For reproducibility, the overall optimization procedure of DrunkAgent is given in Algorithm 1. For the sake of algorithmic transparency, our code will be made available after this work is published.
A.2 Dataset Statistics
Table 2 illustrates the statistics of these datasets. The datasets vary in size and sparsity, which is suitable for a comprehensive evaluation. Following the work [50], we further sample subsets from the datasets given the expensive API calls. Specifically, considering the importance and commonness of data sparsity and cold-start in the recommendation community, we randomly sample 100 users with 800 interactions (777 items), 99 users with 693 interactions (619 items) and 98 users with 588 interactions (483 items) from the three datasets, respectively, allowing to explore more diverse and practical interaction scenarios. Moreover, the leave-one-out strategy [15] is used for evaluation, where the split between the

training and test set is 9:1. Scaling DrunkAgent for larger datasets is left as future work.
A.3 Ablation Studies
We remove important components of each module to conduct ablation studies of DrunkAgent. First, we consider remove the surrogate module (denoted as S ). However, if we remove it, the setting will transfer from the black-box to the white-box that is impractical and beyond the scope of this paper. Moreover, due to gpt-3.5-turbo used for victim models is closed-source and only can be called via APIs, we cannot obtain logits to optimize the adversarial triggers like Llama-3 used in the surrogate model does. Hence, we maintain the surrogate model to ensure more practical settings. We then remove the greedy optimization algorithm (denoted as G ) in the generation module to observe how the performance changes. As illustrated in Table 3, the attack performance moderately degrades after removing G , which demonstrates the effectiveness of the optimization algorithm. The lack of dramatic performance degradation is probably due to the effectiveness of our initialization. Finally, we mask the strategy module (denoted as S ). From the table, we can find that the attack performance decreases by a large margin. This may be because memory updates during agent-environment interactions are somewhat robust to non-tailored attacks (this phenomenon can also be observed in the performance of the attack baselines). Our reported results are built upon AgentCF, and similar tendencies can be found on AgentRAG and AgentSEQ.
A.4 Parameter Sensitivity
From Table 3, we can find that the strategy module contributes more than the optimization algorithm on DrunkAgent's attack performance. As such, we increase the maximum rounds of optimization iterations per step of victim models to evaluate the attack success rates of the attack strategy, where the default value is 2 as suggested in the paper [50]. As illustrated in Table 4, the success rates of getting the target agents drunk are maintained at 100% regardless of the number of iterations. For the other attack parameters, based on our observations, our DrunkAgent is insensitive to them.
A.5 Victim LLM-powered Agentic Models
LLM-driven agents, known for their superior autonomous interaction and decision-making capabilities, are gradually being considered as next-generation RSs. Compared with some agent-powered RSs that focus solely on user-side behavior modeling using universal LLMs [16, 37], AgentCF [50] emphasizes the modeling of two-sided interaction relations between user agents and item agents through the idea of collaborative filtering (CF) [15]. Both kinds of agents are equipped with memory modules, maintaining the simulated preferences and tastes of potential adopters involving their intrinsic features and acquired behavioral information via autonomous interactions and collaborative reflection optimizations at each time step. As such, the memories of the user agents and item agents can be accordingly updated, enabling the agents to better fit the real-world interaction behaviors. To conduct personalized recommendations, AgentCF introduces various well-crafted prompting strategies on the optimized memories. A.6 Baselines
The baselines perturb the descriptions of the target items at various levels, which are described as below.
· Character-level Perturbations. DeepwordBug [12] and PuncAttack [11] manipulate texts by introducing typos and inserting punctuation, respectively.
· Word-level Perturbations. TextFooler [18] and BertAttack [21], respectively, aim to replace words with synonyms or contextually similar words.
· Sentence-level Perturbations. TrivialInsertion [51] inserts text content with  number of positive words from a predefined word corpus, where the insertion is conducted at the end of the text. Here,  = 2. ChatGPTAttack [51] increases the stealthiness of TrivialInsertion by rewriting the text content via GPTs. GPT-3.5-turbo is used and the maximum number of words is set to 30 here.
We give some examples of adversarial descriptions of target items generated by the baselines in Table 6. Following the works [16, 50], the item memory of the agent can be initialized by their identity information, such as titles and categories, as shown in the benign (default) descriptions of the tables. Although the adversarial description of the target item generated from DrunkAgent is different from the benign status's (see Fig. 2 and Table 6), such modifications are feasible due to the real-world item providers is allowed to modify their product representations via APIs [7, 26, 45], as discussed in Section 1, especially when the benign description is just a default exemplar used for experiments. Moreover, compared with the number of queries (denoted as Q in the table) of the baselines, DrunkAgent has the lowest attack costs due to E=20. The implementation and parameters of the baselines follow the open-source works: StealthyAttack6 [51] and PromptBench7 [58].
